# Guide to SLAs

Imagine this: Your team is overwhelmed, tickets are slipping through the cracks, and your customers are frustrated. Agents don’t know which tasks to prioritize, and there’s no clear benchmark for performance improvement. Every day, customer demands increase, and satisfaction steadily drops. Chaos reigns.

In situations like this, service-level agreements (SLAs) can turn things around. SLAs define clear expectations between a company and its service providers. They give business owners peace of mind, while helping service teams avoid misunderstandings about what’s expected. Essentially, an SLA ensures everyone knows what “good service” looks like and how to achieve it.

This article will cover everything you need to know about SLAs: what they are, their benefits and challenges, key components, FAQs, and tips for managing them effectively.

## What is an SLA?

A service-level agreement is a contract between a service provider and a customer that defines:

- The services to be delivered
- Expected response times
- Performance metrics used to measure success

SLAs make it easier to track team performance and clearly communicate service expectations to customers.

**Example:**

Imagine you work at an internet service provider. Customers expect certain download and upload speeds, uptime guarantees, and timely resolution of issues. For instance, a P1-level incident may need to be resolved within four hours, 24/7. That commitment is an SLA, which is more than just a document. It’s a promise to your customers.

SLAs can exist between an organization and its external customers or internally between departments. Defining them during the service design phase is critical, as they can influence software architecture, IT operations, and overall customer satisfaction.

## What’s inside an SLA?

At its core, an SLA defines a measurable target (such as response time, uptime, or resolution time) and the corresponding goal for meeting it. Beyond this, an SLA typically includes:

- A description of the services provided
- Expected service levels for each service
- Metrics used to measure performance
- Roles and responsibilities of each party
- Remedies or penalties for missed targets
- Guidelines for updating or revising the SLA

Common SLA metrics include:

- Response time for support requests
- Service availability and uptime
- Maintenance windows and downtime
- Disaster recovery procedures

The ultimate goal of an SLA is to ensure consistent service delivery. Aligning SLA metrics with business objectives is key. Penalties for missed SLAs, which can often be monetary, create incentives for providers to meet targets, ensuring accountability.
